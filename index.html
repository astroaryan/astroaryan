Brown Dwarf Secondary Eclipses

We studied two transiting brown dwarfs, LP 261-75C and AD 3116B, using the 200-inch Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory. Brown dwarfs have roughly 13–80 times Jupiter's mass, too little to sustain hydrogen fusion like a star. These two orbit their host stars about every two days; each star is roughly five times more massive than its companion.

A transit happens when a brown dwarf crosses in front of its star from our viewpoint. When it passes behind the star, we see a secondary eclipse: a small dip in the system's light as the brown dwarf disappears from view.

We used these dips to estimate how brightly the brown dwarfs glow. Because brown dwarfs cool as they age, comparing our measurements with cooling models lets us estimate the ages of their systems. The models suggest 0.4–1.3 billion years for AD 3116B and 1.3–2.9 billion years for LP 261-75C, with the ranges reflecting differences between models.
